How I think about design

I start from what I want players to understand, and build it into the rules of the game.

If players need to understand both the money side of a farm and what it means for the families who run it, I write the rules so that they can only do well by weighing both. That thinking then shows in the choices they make, and the discussion after the game helps them put it into words.

What my PhD taught me

People understand the whole problem best when three things come together.

For my PhD I studied six of my games, played by 472 people in Belgium, Nigeria and Uganda. People explained how the whole problem fits together best when the game gave them:

So I plan all three on purpose: what stays on the table, the moment the plan should fail, and the questions for the discussion afterwards.

The research in more depth

After the game: conditions of play for systems-oriented explanation in public health and One Health education. PhD in Public Health, University of Liège, since 2021 (defence 2027), with Prof. Nicolas Antoine-Moussiaux. Six games, 2021–2024, 472 adults. I analysed what players said and wrote in the discussion after each game, and compared it with how each game was set up.

Three kinds of material. Given: what I supply, such as a board or cards. Made: what players build, such as a map or overlay. Kept: what is recorded during play to reconstruct from, such as a log or a photo of the board. Many do more than one job.

Two findings that changed my design. No single game helped players both follow how things change over time and question how the problem had been defined. So I now combine different games, or add a second round where players can rethink their choices. Questionnaires filled in alone captured less than the same people explained together, so I give them after the group discussion.

Mongolia · 2025–now · One Health training of trainers

Training the people who will train others

  1. The goalHelp Mongolian specialists in public health, veterinary science, wildlife, environment and agriculture see how herders' livelihoods, livestock, wildlife and rangelands depend on each other, and pass this on with tools they can use themselves.
  2. The designOnline One Health modules built on Mongolian cases, such as plague in marmots, harsh dzud winters, and herders living next to mines. Then on-site training where every participatory tool was tried on a problem from the participants' own work.
  3. What happenedParticipants said the systems ideas became clear once they had tried the tools themselves. As trainers, they now carry them to herders, health workers, students and local officials.

  • Online modules and on-site training
  • With the Government of Mongolia

Bangladesh · 2024 · Friendship · evaluation with communities

Teaching a field team to evaluate with communities

  1. The goalEvaluate a six-year project (2017–2023, 198 communities in five districts) together with the people who lived it, so their own account of what changed is at the centre.
  2. The designWe trained a team of local data collectors, all social science graduates, to run interviews and group discussions and to use participatory tools such as social maps, timelines and proportional piling, entering data live on their phones.
  3. What happenedCommunities drew maps and timelines of what had changed for them. Their accounts shaped the final report and its recommendations, for example on women's leadership and a community contingency fund.

Senegal · 2026 · One Health territory game · run by the partners

Three sectors, one map

  1. The goalHealth, farming and livestock teams each see the area through their own lens. The goal is for them to notice this, and to see that the problems cross from one sector to another.
  2. The rulesInformation costs money and each team can afford only part of it. Teams draw what they learn on clear plastic sheets laid over the same map. Most actions need several teams to pay together, and in a second round teams can change their choices.
  3. What happenedThe partners ran the workshop in Senegal on their own, using only the materials. The photos below show the teams' own sheets and maps: stacked, they reveal links between sectors, and the discussion asks whose knowledge was missing.
  • Game designer
  • Cards, map, clear sheets · 3 h 45
  • DAETOX / TISS · Ucoopia, IRD
More on this game

Who plays: agronomists, health and veterinary staff, local officials and producers, in three sector teams. Funding: FFEM, DGD, Colruyt Group Foundation; TISS by Wallonie-Bruxelles International. Partners include LRIDA (Université de Parakou) and the ULiège Tropical Veterinary Institute.

Each team's list of what to find out next goes straight into the field study that follows the game.

Belgium · 2024–2025 · chronic care · Province of Luxembourg

A paper tool care teams run themselves

  1. The goalHelp health and care professionals see a care problem as a chain of causes across services, and agree on first actions they can take with what they have.
  2. The designBuilt with the practitioners from five years of their own interviews and focus groups. Three steps on one large sheet: draw the current care pathway and pick one obstacle; link factor cards with arrows to see what drives it; choose the actions that will move it most. We trained the practitioners to run it themselves.
  3. What happenedAt the programme's closing day in December 2024, health professionals worked in small groups, mapped the causes of their obstacles with the cards and named first actions.
  • Co-designer and trainer
  • Paper tool in three steps
  • About 60 health professionals

Belgium · 2022–now · board game · 450+ players

An island everyone can ruin

  1. The goalWhen everyone takes a little from a shared resource, small gains add up to a large shared loss over time. The goal is for players to see this happen.
  2. The rulesTeams score points by building houses. Each house adds waste to land everyone shares, and the forest cleans up less of it each year. Teams may help each other, but nobody tells them so. There are three sessions, with a discussion after each.
  3. What happenedOver the three sessions, groups stopped protecting their own score and started protecting the island, and tried that new approach in the next session.
  • Co-developer and facilitator
  • 450+ players · ULiège, UCLouvain, ULB
  • Published 2025
Where it is played

Played at the University of Liège, UCLouvain and ULB, and at a summer school, with more than 450 players so far. Evaluated with 70 students through written reflections, focus groups and observation; published in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2025.

How I work with partners

Who plays comes first.

  1. Who plays, and where? (room, devices, time)
  2. What should they understand?
  3. Which rules make them think about it?
  4. Build it fast, with AI
  5. Someone else runs it; I watch
  6. Cut what wastes their time

Partners receive a design file, game cards produced from a spreadsheet so the numbers always add up, rule sheets for each stage, a minute-by-minute guide for the facilitator, a plan for testing the game, and the questions for the discussion afterwards.

Working together

What I bring

  • Games built around what decision-makers need to understand before they decide.
  • Games that partners can run on their own, with everything they need in the box.
  • Years of teaching and facilitating, from lecture halls to villages, so every design starts from the room it will be used in.
  • A discussion after the game that is planned as carefully as the game itself, so what players work out feeds into real decisions.
  • Work in English and French, on paper or online, with a clear account of what each game can and cannot show.
