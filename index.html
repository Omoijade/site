How I think about design

I start from what I want players to understand, and build it into the rules of the game.

If players need to understand both the money side of a farm and what it means for the families who run it, I write the rules so that they can only do well by weighing both. That thinking then shows in the choices they make, and the discussion after the game helps them put it into words.

What my PhD taught me

People understand the whole problem best when three things come together.

For my PhD I studied six of my games, played by 472 people in Belgium, Nigeria and Uganda. People explained how the whole problem fits together best when the game gave them:

So I plan all three on purpose: what stays on the table, the moment the plan should fail, and the questions for the discussion afterwards.

The research in more depth

After the game: conditions of play for systems-oriented explanation in public health and One Health education. PhD in Public Health, University of Liège, since 2021 (defence 2027), with Prof. Nicolas Antoine-Moussiaux. Six games, 2021–2024, 472 adults. I analysed what players said and wrote in the discussion after each game, and compared it with how each game was set up.

Three kinds of material. Given: what I supply, such as a board or cards. Made: what players build, such as a map or overlay. Kept: what is recorded during play to reconstruct from, such as a log or a photo of the board. Many do more than one job.

Two findings that changed my design. No single game helped players both follow how things change over time and question how the problem had been defined. So I now combine different games, or add a second round where players can rethink their choices. Questionnaires filled in alone captured less than the same people explained together, so I give them after the group discussion.

Senegal · 2026 · One Health territory game · run by the partners

Three sectors, one map

  1. The goalHealth, farming and livestock teams each see the area through their own lens. The goal is for them to notice this, and to see that the problems cross from one sector to another.
  2. The rulesInformation costs money and each team can afford only part of it. Teams draw what they learn on clear plastic sheets laid over the same map. Most actions need several teams to pay together, and in a second round teams can change their choices.
  3. What happenedThe partners ran the workshop in Senegal on their own, using only the materials. The photos below show the teams' own sheets and maps: stacked, they reveal links between sectors, and the discussion asks whose knowledge was missing.
  • Game designer
  • Cards, map, clear sheets · 3 h 45
  • DAETOX / TISS · Ucoopia, IRD
More on this game

Who plays: agronomists, health and veterinary staff, local officials and producers, in three sector teams. Funding: FFEM, DGD, Colruyt Group Foundation; TISS by Wallonie-Bruxelles International. Partners include LRIDA (Université de Parakou) and the ULiège Tropical Veterinary Institute.

Each team's list of what to find out next goes straight into the field study that follows the game.

Belgium · 2022–now · board game · 450+ players

An island everyone can ruin

  1. The goalWhen everyone takes a little from a shared resource, small gains add up to a large shared loss over time. The goal is for players to see this happen.
  2. The rulesTeams score points by building houses. Each house adds waste to land everyone shares, and the forest cleans up less of it each year. Teams may help each other, but nobody tells them so. There are three sessions, with a discussion after each.
  3. What happenedOver the three sessions, groups stopped protecting their own score and started protecting the island, and tried that new approach in the next session.

Belgium, France, Canada · 2026 · browser simulation built with AI

A clinic that checks your reasons

  1. The goalSee a veterinary clinic as a whole, where money, access to care, staff wellbeing and carbon emissions all affect each other, and judge a decision by comparing it with what would have happened without it.
  2. The rulesTeams make only three decisions a year, so they can follow cause and effect. Every plan is shown next to a "do nothing" version, and every decision is recorded so it can be replayed.
  3. What happenedOne team said hiring a new vet had earned €58,600. When we replayed the clinic without her, the real gain was about €28,000. Hiring her was still a good choice, but not for the reason they gave.
  • Designer and developer
  • English and French
  • Used at 4 other universities
More on this simulation

A model of 14 services, staff time, loans, commitments to five groups of people and carbon from published emission factors, with a workbook and teacher guides. Built with an AI model through a prompt structure I designed. First run 19–23 October 2026 with three teams. It is now used at four other universities, in France and Canada. A sister simulation runs a dairy farm one year per turn.

DR Congo, Nigeria, Mongolia · 2025–now · livestock, antibiotics, rangelands

Maps people draw themselves

  1. The goalThe people who live in a place hold much of the knowledge a decision needs, and choosing what to find out already shapes the answer. The goal is for participants to experience both.
  2. The rulesParticipants draw the map of their own area themselves. Information has a price, and the budget covers only part of it. The finished map is kept and used in the discussion afterwards.
  3. What happenedIn a territory mapping game in Kinshasa, a cholera group and a malaria group drew two very different maps of the same area. On a Nigerian campus, students traced how leftover medicines travel from student rooms to the rubbish dump.

Belgium · 2025–now · public health · Google Sheets and phones

A crisis game for a full lecture hall

  1. The goalPlayers weigh saving lives against public trust and fairness as an epidemic spreads from one commune to the next. My own design question: how do you run a real game in a lecture hall of about a hundred students, with fixed seats, their phones and no budget?
  2. The rulesEach group runs a commune and votes by phone through a Google Form. A shared Google Sheet spreads the epidemic to neighbouring communes, shows lives, trust and fairness on the big screen, and keeps every vote. Prevention takes time to work, control works at once, and a solidarity fund lets communes help the most fragile. One facilitator runs it all from a single menu.
  3. What happenedIn the first version, two rows put all their money into police control in the last round. Because every vote was recorded, the class could see exactly when it turned, and discuss what that cost in public trust.
  • Designer and developer
  • Free tools, one facilitator
  • Built for large lecture halls
More on this game and ROOM

The first version, Budget de confiance, ran on 21 November 2025: eight rows of students, six rounds, 42 votes recorded. The current version, Cellule de crise, has thirteen communes of about eight students each.

ROOM is a meeting tool I built with a related goal: making sure a meeting ends with clear agreements. One topic at a time, one clock, a check before moving on, and commitments with an owner and a date. In use since January 2026, including with the Eklipse working group and the Province de Hainaut.

A guide for designing games for policy

  1. The goalGive game makers a guide to designing for what is needed at each level of biodiversity policy (local, regional, national) and at each stage of the policy cycle.
  2. How we workWith a group of about 14 experts, we are writing a methods protocol: a review of published games, a close look at selected game cases, and workshops where the experts compare where games help most. The guide comes from the group, so how we work together matters as much as the method.
  3. What comes nextThe workshops run in 2027 and lead to a report with design guidance for game makers.

Working together

What I bring

  • Games built around what decision-makers need to understand before they decide.
  • Games that partners can run on their own, with everything they need in the box.
  • A discussion after the game that is planned as carefully as the game itself, so what players work out feeds into real decisions.
  • Work in English and French, on paper or online, with a clear account of what each game can and cannot show.
