Doctoral research · 2020–2026

The dissertation

"After the game: conditions of play and systems-oriented explanation in public health and One Health education"

The research asks how serious games and participatory activities, used as learning environments, shape what participants make explicit as systems-oriented reasoning once the activity ends. Six cases, run between 2020 and 2024 with 0 adult participants across community, university, veterinary, medical, public health, and One Health settings, are compared through a Context-Mechanism-Outcome (CMO) lens: the setting each activity ran in, what made explanation possible inside it, and what participants could then say about the system they had engaged with.

Three conditions recur across the six cases.

→ →

A shared artefact is an object or exercise participants can point to and build on. It differs by authorship: it can be encoded in the design, assembled by participants, or made during play.

Friction is a source of resistance participants encounter. It reaches them from three directions: placed by the design, arising from how the activity is used, or brought in by the participants themselves.

A reconstruction space is a moment set aside for participants to work back through what happened. What it asks decides what the reasoning reaches — the same artefact and friction can lead to different explanations depending on the prompt.

Each condition is necessary on its own. None is sufficient alone.

Case 01

Pandemic

Mixed disciplines, four cohorts.

Published as a serious game on the 4Cs of One Health under pressure, in Scientific Reports, 2026. Compared against Tipping Point, the difference between the two cases turns on which relationship became the group's shared object.

Case 02

Ibadan mapping

Twelve community members, one session.

Part of a water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) co-design published in One Health Cases, 2023.

Case 03

Territory Game

156 veterinary students, 88 written responses.

The case that resists the general reading: all three conditions were present, and the explanation participants gave stayed with the activity itself, because the reconstruction prompts asked about the activity.

Case 04

Tipping Point

Five cohorts, three sessions each.

The flagship, most fully evaluated case in the comparison, played by more than 450 participants.

Case 05

Micro-warrior

42 students in focus groups, five universities.

The artefact here was availability rather than an object: scenes participants remembered from the game, assembled after the fact through discussion.

Case 06

Avian Game

74 veterinary students.

One of two veterinary-student cases in the comparison, alongside Territory Game.

Design practice

Games

Alongside the dissertation, Julian designs and tests games built on the same three conditions: artefact, friction, and reconstruction space.

Flagship

Tipping Point

Developed with a full facilitation process, a physical prototype, and a digital adaptation, and played by more than 0 participants. A mixed-method study of 70 of them appeared in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications in 2025.

Veterinary clinic simulation

A management simulation paired with a five-day pedagogical module at the University of Liège, built around Ainsworth's DeFT framework for multiple external representations. The module runs Monday to Friday, scaffolding systems thinking across the week alongside the simulation itself.

A new prototype, still in design, tests the same three conditions against land-use trade-offs between farming, conservation, and public health.

Field and collaborative work

Where the work has run

Nigeria

A case study for CABI's One Health Cases on a youth-led antimicrobial resistance (AMR) mapping exercise, run by the AMR Club at Ladoke Akintola University of Technology during World AMR Awareness Week 2025. Thirty-seven student volunteers mapped 74 risk points across campus and built a public mural and digital exhibit from the results. A parallel exercise, run independently, produced comparable results at the University of Nigeria, Nsukka.

Mongolia

An international training of trainers on One Health under the Resilient Threads programme, run with the Odyssey Conservation Trust, the Sustainable Fibre Alliance, the Royal Veterinary College, the University of Liège, and the Government of Mongolia, covering herder livelihoods, livestock health, wildlife, rangeland condition, climate pressure, food security, and the cashmere supply chain.

Bangladesh

Working with FRIENDSHIP Bangladesh and FRIENDSHIP Luxembourg, training local facilitators in social mapping, timeline construction, and proportional piling, a method for ranking priorities with counters, for an evaluation covering 0 families across 198 climate-vulnerable communities. The analysis fed an independent University of Liège evaluation and a policy brief.

Uganda

Design and evaluation of a digital narrative game on antimicrobial stewardship, built with medical and nursing students from five Ugandan universities.

He has also contributed the qualitative data analysis to multi-author policy studies on rabies prioritisation in Burkina Faso, avian influenza decision-making in Ivory Coast, zoonotic disease data sharing in Senegal, and rabies coordination in the Philippines.

Musings

Notes along the way

On tracking less

Design note

Early drafts of The Visit tracked everything: capacity units, demand types, a running tally after each round. Play slowed to bookkeeping. I removed the trackers and asked players to say out loud what they were short on. Interdependence stopped being a number on a sheet and became a conversation between roles. A tracker automates a judgment a person should make out loud.

What counts as an artefact

Field note

Micro-warrior's focus groups worked from an artefact that never sat on the table: scenes participants remembered from the game itself, assembled after the fact through discussion. An artefact does not need to be an object placed in front of a group. It needs to be available, something participants can return to and build on together.

The case that didn't fit

Research note

Territory Game sat inside the same architecture as the other five cases: a shared artefact, friction, a space set aside to reconstruct. The explanation participants gave still stayed with the activity itself, and the reasoning stopped there. The reconstruction prompts had asked about the activity. What a debrief asks decides what a group can say.
